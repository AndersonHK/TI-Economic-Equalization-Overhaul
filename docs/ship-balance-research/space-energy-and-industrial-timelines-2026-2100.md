# Space energy, propulsion, and lunar industry: conditional timelines to 2100

Research date: 2026-09-20. Research and interpretation only; no gameplay, executable data, or tooling changes.

Supporting [working primary-source notebook](details/space-energy-primary-source-notes-2026-09-20.md) preserves brief verbatim excerpts, exact source locations, version boundaries, and interpretations as the reading progresses.

The central judgment is that small space fission systems are credible near-term projects, fusion pilot electricity before 2040 is plausible, and lightweight fusion propulsion is a separate, substantially less mature objective. Advanced gas-core fission is not a prerequisite for fusion. Fission may remain competitive in space long after fusion starts supplying terrestrial electricity.

## Scope and evidence

The research began with the MTS interview [NASA Chief: How To Reach Another Star System | Jared Isaacman](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7_ci-2zoMH8), published September 17, 2026. The title and publication date were checked on the original YouTube page. Its English auto-generated captions were exported and read, including the relevant passages below. Numeric transcription errors are possible; this report does not treat the captioned energy-conversion percentages as technical specifications.

The approach is to trace statements to agency documents, original technical papers, laboratory results, and companies' own disclosures; distinguish observations from plans; then build conditional scenarios. Company announcements establish what companies claim and intend, not independent verification. No contract-by-contract funding audit or independent plasma-performance replication was performed. Sources were accessed September 20, 2026. Search-engine crawl dates were not treated as publication dates.

"Demonstrated" means the stated experiment was reported as performed. "Program target" means a dated agency objective. "Company target" means a developer's forecast. "Concept" means analysis or proposed hardware. Timeline ranges below are this report's judgments, not statistical confidence intervals or source promises. They include the possibility that a branch never becomes useful.

I interpret "internal confinement" as **inertial confinement fusion (ICF)**. It is one candidate for advanced propulsion, not an established final stage above magnetic confinement. If inertial electrostatic confinement was intended, that is a different and considerably less validated power-producing branch.

## The primary-source trail behind the offhand remarks

### Space nuclear power

The [NASA–DOE January 13, 2026 announcement](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-department-of-energy-to-develop-lunar-surface-reactor-by-2030/) establishes a lunar-reactor objective for 2030. This is supported by an interagency agreement, not merely an interview aspiration.

NASA's [March 2026 nuclear-space presentation](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/5-america-underway-in-space-on-nuclear-power.pdf), slides 4, 7, 11 and 12, describes SR-1 Freedom with **greater than 20 kWe**, HALEU uranium-dioxide fuel, heat pipes, Brayton conversion, and electric propulsion. It targets December 2028, followed by LR-1 in 2030 and hundreds of kWe to MW-class systems in the 2030s. Its **up-to-48-kW thruster-system label is not a 48-kWe reactor rating**. The [May 22 directive](https://www.nasa.gov/blogs/workforce-updates/2026/05/22/a-message-from-administrator-jared-isaacman/) states SR-1 targets 2028 and LR-1 readiness for launch by 2030. Readiness, launch, landing, commissioning, and dependable service are distinct milestones.

The [August 2025 industry request](https://www.nasa.gov/centers-and-facilities/glenn/nasa-seeks-industry-feedback-on-fission-surface-power/) sought at least 100 kWe. An [older project page](https://www.nasa.gov/exploration-systems-development-mission-directorate/fission-surface-power/) still describes a 40-kW class system. These are evolving program baselines. Do not combine their most favorable specifications into an imaginary finalized reactor.

### Lunar manufacture and mass drivers

The clearest documentary match is [SpaceX's June 5, 2026 EU prospectus](https://content.spacex.com/cms-assets/FINAL_Documents%20and%20Updates/SpaceX%20-%20EU%20Prospectus%20%28Approved%20by%20Bafin%29%20-%20June%205%2C%202026.pdf), PDF page 95. It explicitly connects extraction, local manufacture of much of a satellite's mass, imported chips, and mass-driver launch. The [company interview filed with the SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1181412/000162828026041761/fwp_cfointerview.htm) similarly describes lunar production of photovoltaics and radiators. These establish an industrial strategy; they do not establish profitable throughput or an operating date.

There is also an actual manufacturing precursor: NASA reports [oxygen and metal production from regolith simulant in a vacuum chamber](https://www.nasa.gov/centers-and-facilities/kennedy/nasa-kennedy-breathes-life-into-moon-soil-testing/). Blue Origin reports a [2025 Blue Alchemist critical design review](https://www.blueorigin.com/news/blue-alchemist-hits-major-milestone-toward-permanent-sustainable-lunar-infrastructure), with development supported by NASA, for a chain that includes oxygen, metals, and solar-cell materials. These are Earth-based development results, not lunar factories. The resource-extraction test apparatus also called LR-1 must not be confused with NASA's Lunar Reactor-1.

For the historical engineering lineage, [NASA SP-428](https://history.arc.nasa.gov/hist_pdfs/nasa_sp428.pdf) and the [Mass Driver 2 report](https://libraryssi.org/flips/19820800_md2_snow1/) document analyses and prototypes. SpaceX's particular implementation is not established by those older designs.

## Why NERVA did not produce a steady technological ladder

The old results were substantial. The [1968 federal space report](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/presrep1968.pdf) records Phoebus-2A tests up to 4,200 MW. That is **thermal power in a ground-tested rocket reactor**, not continuous electric output. Hydrogen carries the heat out through the exhaust. A comparably rated electric station would need conversion equipment and a way to reject its unconverted heat.

More recent evidence includes the [2018 KRUSTY demonstration of a 1-kWe-class design](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20200001569/downloads/20200001569.pdf), and NASA's [2025 cold-flow development-unit tests, reported in 2026](https://www.nasa.gov/directorates/stmd/tech-demo-missions-program/space-nuclear-propulsion-snp/nasa-testing-advances-space-nuclear-propulsion-capabilities/). The latter used a full-scale **non-nuclear** article; they were not a hot firing of a completed nuclear rocket.

My interpretation is that the long gap reflects both interrupted development and demanding engineering. A hot reactor experiment does not provide flight qualification, fuel manufacture at scale, long-duration hydrogen storage, payload integration, maintenance economics, or a mission customer. Funding can change the order: NASA's [FY2026 budget technical supplement](https://nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/fy-2026-budget-technical-supplement-002.pdf) proposed no funding for nuclear thermal or electric propulsion, whereas its later 2026 announcements prioritize nuclear flight demonstrations. A budget request is not an enacted appropriation, but the contrast illustrates program instability.

Gas-core propulsion adds major technical risks. Keeping gaseous nuclear fuel confined while heating and exhausting hydrogen is an integrated fluid, radiation, materials, and neutronics problem. The [1971 high-specific-impulse gas-core study](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19710010425) contains calculated designs at 3,000–7,000 seconds specific impulse; it is not evidence of engines operating at those values. Better computation can guide experiments without replacing them.

Consequently, first useful fusion electricity could plausibly arrive before a useful gas-core rocket. A successful nuclear-thermal rocket could also precede either. Neither ordering violates physics.

## What has actually changed in fusion

| Track | Public evidence or stated objective | Remaining distinction |
|---|---|---|
| High-field tokamak | [SPARC model-coil paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.12301) reports a 20.1-T peak conductor-field test. [SPARC's current page](https://www.cfs.energy/technology/sparc/) targets plasma gain in 2027. | A magnet demonstration and a planned gain experiment do not constitute net plant electricity. |
| ARC | [CFS targets approximately 400 MWe in the early 2030s](https://www.cfs.energy/technology/arc/). | Developer target requiring an integrated plant. |
| Optimized stellarator | [W7-X's 2025 campaign](https://www.ipp.mpg.de/5532474/w7x) achieved a long-pulse triple-product record over 43 seconds. | A confinement result, not 43 seconds of net electricity or an ignited D-T reactor. |
| Helios | [Thea's September 9, 2026 announcement](https://thea.energy/press-release/thea-energy-publishes-the-most-practical-fusion-power-plant-design-and-a-clear-path-toward-energy-on-the-grid/) reports a published design series for an approximately 400-MWe stellarator. | Design and analysis, not operating performance. Helios is Thea's plant, distinct from Helion. |
| Pulsed FRC/direct conversion | [Helion's February 2026 report](https://www.helionenergy.com/newsroom/helion-achieves-new-fusion-energy-milestones) describes 150-million-degree plasma and measurable D-T fusion. [Orion targets](https://www.helionenergy.com/orion) initial operation in 2028 and at least 50 MW under its purchase agreement. | Those reported measurements alone do not demonstrate net exported electricity. Fuel strategy and complete energy accounting remain decisive. |
| Inertial confinement | [LLNL reports](https://str.llnl.gov/str-julyaugust-2025/future-ignition) an April 2025 shot with 8.6 MJ fusion yield from 2.08 MJ laser energy at the target. | Target gain 4.13 excludes upstream laser losses and plant auxiliaries. |
| ITER | [Updated baseline](https://www.iter.org/few-lines): research operation in 2034, full magnetic energy in 2036, D-T operation in 2039. | ITER is an experiment and will not export electricity. Other approaches need not wait for its completion. |

The strongest reason to revise the old "always thirty years away" view is the combination of actual target ignition, stronger practical magnets, better validated confinement design, and multiple funded development approaches. The strongest reason not to accept developers' dates literally is that most remaining difficulties interact inside the complete plant.

The international comparison strengthens the case for overlapping timelines. [UKAEA describes STEP as an early-2040s pilot](https://www.ukaea.org/news/response-to-daily-telegraph-article/), while [ITER's program overview](https://www.iter.org/fusion-energy/after-iter) places European DEMO around 2050 and describes China's BEST construction objective for 2027. [Proxima's February 2026 agreement](https://www.proximafusion.com/press-news/proxima-fusion-rwe-the-free-state-of-bavaria-and-max-planck-institute-for-plasma-physics-sign-agreement-to-build-the-worlds-first-commercial-fusion-power-plant-in-europe) distinguishes the Alpha stellarator gain demonstrator from the subsequent Stellaris power plant. These are separate programs with different objectives and risk tolerances. A 2050 DEMO date is not evidence that no earlier plant can work; an early gain target is not evidence that a fleet of durable plants follows immediately.

There are at least four different milestones:

1. **Plasma or target gain:** fusion output exceeds a specified input to plasma or target.
2. **Net plant electricity:** electricity exported after drivers, heaters, cryogenics, pumps, fuel processing, and other operating loads.
3. **Commercial service:** useful availability, component life, maintenance, fuel supply, cost, and repeatable construction.
4. **Spacecraft usefulness:** favorable mass, volume, acceleration, radiation environment, thermal rejection, and mission reliability.

A short experiment can pass the first and remain far from the fourth. A specialized propulsion experiment could conversely precede economically competitive terrestrial generation: it has a different objective and may discharge energy in propellant. There is no mandatory commercial-grid-first sequence, but present investment and published designs make early terrestrial plants the stronger central expectation.

The [June 2026 DOE roadmap](https://www.energy.gov/documents/fusion-science-and-technology-roadmap) explicitly identifies gaps in materials, integrated blankets, tritium processing, and whole-plant engineering. It places integrated fuel self-sufficiency and power-plant-relevant blanket validation among later milestones. Its schedule is conditional on resources, not a funded guarantee. For the D-T fuel constraint, the [IAEA's tritium discussion](https://nucleus.iaea.org/sites/connect/FUSEpublic/SitePages/Tritium-Breeding.aspx) explains why substantial natural supply is unavailable and lithium breeding is needed.

AI can improve control, optimization, simulation, and experiment selection; robotics can improve repeatability and remote maintenance. An actual example is [LLNL's laser-control work](https://lift.llnl.gov/using-ai-real-time-control-rapid-fire-lasers). My assessment is that these can shorten learning cycles materially. They do not establish the lifetime of a component that has not accumulated representative neutron exposure, or close a fuel balance through software alone. Higher test throughput is more informative than claims of exponential progress.

## Scale: distinguish the plasma, reactor, power plant, and spacecraft

There is no single meaningful answer to "how large can the reactor be?" The important measurements are power type, duty cycle, complete system mass, and useful delivered performance.

| Scale | Plausible role | Evidential boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Tens of kWe, then approximately 100 kWe | Surface survival, communications, instruments, pilot resource processing; small nuclear-electric spacecraft | Near-term program objectives; integration and flight still matter. |
| 0.1–1 MWe | Larger outposts, processing campaigns, sustained cargo propulsion | Plausible next development range, not demonstrated fleet capability. |
| 1–10 MWe space systems | Larger industrial loads and interplanetary freight | Conditional 2030s–2050s development; radiator and specific-mass improvement required. |
| Tens to hundreds of MWe in space | Extensive industry and higher-performance large transports | Substantial industrial infrastructure; no defensible near-term promise. |
| Hundreds of MW to GW thermal, short burns | Nuclear-thermal propulsion | Historical ground power scales already reach here; flight readiness is separate. |
| Roughly 50–400 MWe terrestrial fusion plants | Pilot/commercial electricity objectives | Announced developer scales, not demonstrated commercial plants or spacecraft packages. |
| GW-class continuous jet power | Large fast transports, if mass and heat rejection permit | Far-future conditional capability. A GW plasma rating alone is insufficient. |

A useful mass anchor comes from the [2021 National Academies assessment](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25977/chapter/5), which treats **20 kg/kWe** for a complete MW-class nuclear-electric propulsion system as a challenging development requirement. That converts to **20 tonnes per MWe**, counting the nuclear/electric propulsion equipment rather than the entire ship. It is a requirement, not a demonstrated modern reactor mass. It cannot be compared directly with kilograms per thermal MW for an open-cycle nuclear rocket.

Calculated illustration: at 60% electrical-to-jet efficiency and 30-km/s exhaust, 1 MWe supplies 40 N. On a 100-tonne spacecraft that initially gives 0.0004 m/s². Holding mass and thrust fixed, accumulating 10 km/s takes about 289 days; propellant consumption and orbital dynamics alter an actual trajectory. Increasing exhaust to 100 km/s at the same power lowers thrust to 12 N. This is why small NEP can be excellent for patient freight or robotic exploration without providing fast crew transfers. [A 2025 Saturn/Enceladus study](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44205-025-00145-x) illustrates useful modeled missions in the 10–40-kWe range while explicitly leaving lifetime and specific-mass validation to future development.

For a concrete size anchor, the [December 2025 Helios preconceptual overview, Table 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08027v1) specifies an 8-m major radius, 500 m³ plasma volume, 6-T on-axis field, up to 20 T on coils, and at least 1.2 m plasma-to-coil separation for blanket/shielding. It models 958 MW fusion, 1.1 GW thermal including blanket contributions, and 390 MWe net. These are versioned design figures. The full plant is considerably larger than the plasma. Predicted lifetime and breeding performance are not measured operating records.

Higher fields can raise plasma power density approximately as B^4 at fixed beta, temperature, and composition. That does not make all equipment shrink by the same factor. Neutron shielding, coil forces, cryogenics, plasma exhaust, and maintenance access persist. At fixed output, shrinking the enclosing surface increases loading per unit area. See the separate [magnet-scaling analysis](fusion-magnet-scaling-and-technology-timeline-2026-09-11.md) for the derivation and its assumptions.

Heat rejection illustrates the space penalty. With emissivity 0.9 and an ideal deep-space sink, the Stefan–Boltzmann relation gives the following **calculated total emitting areas per GW of waste heat**:

| Radiator temperature | Emitting area |
|---|---:|
| 800 K | 47,839 m² |
| 1,000 K | 19,595 m² |
| 1,500 K | 3,871 m² |

A two-sided ideal panel has half that planform area. Real view factors, temperature gradients, plumbing and damage tolerance add burdens. Radiator temperature is not plasma temperature. Direct exhaust can remove much of the energy without radiators, but residual neutron, radiation and auxiliary loads still require accounting.

D-T puts approximately 80% of reaction energy into neutrons, which a magnetic nozzle cannot directly steer; see the reaction energies in [IAEA Fundamentals of Magnetic Fusion Technology](https://www-pub.iaea.org/MTCD/Publications/PDF/PUB1945_web.pdf). Heating expendable propellant with neutron energy can change the trade. D–helium-3 and proton–boron offer attractive charged-particle pathways, but harder plasma conditions and fuel/loss problems prevent treating them as an automatic next upgrade. D–helium-3 systems also have neutron-producing side reactions. Lunar helium-3 mining is not a prerequisite for D-T fusion.

## Three conditional timelines

These are ordered scenarios, not three fixed release schedules. Local power and cheap transport are complementary: even a superb engine cannot supply missing landing pads, excavation equipment, or a market. Likewise terrestrial fusion does not automatically accelerate lunar construction. Surface solar, storage and imported fission fuel can support lunar industry without fusion.

| Period | Faster development | Central planning case | Slower development |
|---|---|---|---|
| **2026–2031** | Fission flight and lunar demonstrations near announced dates; strong fusion-gain results and perhaps a first limited net-electric demonstration; robotic extraction pilots. | Fission integration and first flight around the turn of the decade or early 2030s; fusion experiments advance faster than complete power plants; local resource demonstrations remain small. | Space-reactor schedules slip into the 2030s; fusion results improve but no persuasive integrated electricity demonstration; launch/landing bottlenecks limit lunar equipment. |
| **2031–2040** | Repeatable hundreds-of-kWe/MWe fission systems; first fusion plants deliver meaningful power; surface manufacturing and a modest lunar launcher demonstration; NTP if missions justify it. | Early fusion-electric demonstrations around 2035–2045; larger fission outposts and electric cargo stages; oxygen/metals processing serves local users before major exports; nuclear-thermal flight is possible. | Fission works in narrow missions; fusion's blankets, maintenance and economics delay practical service toward 2045–2060; lunar mining remains subsidized pilot activity. |
| **2040–2060** | Commercial terrestrial fusion expands; high-specific-power propulsion branch could demonstrate fusion thrust in the 2040s–2050s; viable lunar manufacturing supports export infrastructure. | Fusion plants become useful on Earth; fission remains a main space option; early fusion-drive demonstrations become plausible in the 2050s–2070s; mass-driver trade develops where customers justify it. | Chemical/solar/fission architectures continue to expand capability; fusion electricity emerges unevenly; useful fusion propulsion is still unproven; mass-driver scale depends on demand rather than calendar. |
| **2060–2100** | Fusion propulsion can enable much faster heavy interplanetary logistics, extensive asteroid operations and ambitious outer-system missions. Small interstellar precursor missions may become credible. | Mixed chemical, solar, fission and some fusion systems support a substantial but geographically uneven space economy. Fast crewed outer-system travel remains conditional. | A considerable cislunar economy and some Mars activity remain possible without competitive fusion spacecraft. Large-scale outer-system settlement and interstellar craft remain uncertain or absent. |

The central case is a judgment, not the median of an expert survey. The fast case requires sustained funding, early experimental success, rapid construction, reliable fuel cycles, and a real market. The slow case does not require a failure of fusion physics: poor availability or cost is enough. A still slower tail includes no economically useful fusion even by 2100.

The strongest expected ordering is:

**Cheaper transport and robotic operations → dependable local power → maintained resource-processing plants → repeatable off-world manufacturing → bulk export where justified.**

Alongside that chain: **fusion gain → complete energy balance → durable service**. A propulsion-specific program can branch away before terrestrial commercialization, but must demonstrate its own energy balance and thrust.

Neither industrial mass drivers nor terrestrial fusion need wait for the other. Neither requires gas-core fission. Early fission, early fusion and advanced fission should overlap in any research-based technology tree.

## What each unlocks

**Dependable tens-to-hundreds of kWe** buy continuity: operations during darkness, heating, communications, life support and pilot processing. Reliability over years can be more valuable than a spectacular peak output.

**MW-class surface power plus maintainable robotics** can support a genuine production line. Local oxygen, shielding, landing surfaces, simple structures and eventually electrical infrastructure reduce delivered-mass needs. Water prospecting and extraction are valuable parallel paths, but a resource detection is not a measured economic reserve.

**Nuclear thermal propulsion** can improve high-thrust in-space maneuvers with hydrogen, roughly doubling chemical exhaust velocity in conventional solid-core designs. It still needs bulky hydrogen tanks and storage. It does not produce a corresponding doubling of every mission's payload or speed. [NASA propulsion overview](https://www.nasa.gov/space-technology-mission-directorate/tdm/space-nuclear-propulsion/)

**High-power electric propulsion** buys propellant economy, flexible robotic missions, and freight capability. NASA reported [120-kW lithium-fed MPD prototype operation in February 2026](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/tech-demonstration/nasa-fires-up-powerful-lithium-fed-thruster-for-trips-to-mars/). That is an actual subsystem advance; long lifetime and complete spacecraft power remain separate milestones. High exhaust velocity can already exist without fusion.

**Economical terrestrial fusion** would principally unlock another firm-energy option for Earth industry. Its effect on launch can come through manufacturing, fuels and infrastructure before any fusion engine flies.

**Lightweight direct fusion propulsion**, if achieved, can combine large velocity budgets with better acceleration than heavy electric systems. This needs a favorable complete engine, not merely more energy per kilogram of fuel. NASA's [Direct Fusion Drive study](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20170003126/downloads/20170003126.pdf) modeled 2.5–5 N per MW of fusion power at about 10,000 s specific impulse. Its MW-class scale is a useful alternative to giant pulse engines, but is a concept rather than measured propulsion performance.

**Inertial-fusion propulsion** offers repeated small energy releases and potentially direct coupling to an exhaust. Its tiny targets do not imply tiny laser drivers, pulse-power equipment or magnetic nozzles. LLNL's [driver-development objectives](https://lift.llnl.gov/research-areas/ife/driver-technology) include at least 10% wall-plug efficiency, roughly 10-Hz operation and billion-shot life. Target production, injection, chamber recovery and sustained efficiency must work together. A spacecraft's requirements differ from a terrestrial plant but do not disappear.

For an illustrative thermal ICF plant, driver efficiency 10% and thermal conversion 40% require target gain above 25 just to repay driver electricity, before other loads: 0.10 × 0.40 × G > 1. This is a simplified calculated example, not a universal ignition or profitability threshold. Direct conversion or different drivers change it.

For scale, [Orth's original VISTA report](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc1413261/m2/1/high_res_d/15015945.pdf), May 2003, models a roughly 6,000-ton wet spacecraft and a 100-ton payload for a 145-day Mars roundtrip; its contingency case takes 183 days. The advanced case assumes target gain 1,500, 12% driver efficiency and up to 30 Hz. These are demanding concept assumptions, not achieved performance. The study gives a reason to investigate ICF for large transports without establishing it as the inevitable best engine.

## Lunar mass drivers: attractive energetics, difficult industry

For an ideal 2.38-km/s lunar escape-speed launch, kinetic energy is only **0.787 kWh/kg**. At 50% electrical-to-payload efficiency, continuous 100-kW input corresponds to an upper-bound **1.53 tonnes/day** of accelerated mass. This excludes excavation, refining, packaging, manufacturing, refrigeration, controls, unused capacity and destination capture. It is not net delivered cargo or an estimate of a mine's output.

At that speed, constant acceleration requires about **28.9 km at 10 g**, **2.89 km at 100 g**, or **289 m at 1,000 g**. Thus simple bulk cargo and fragile assembled satellites imply very different systems. Ground prototypes at high acceleration do not establish low-acceleration satellite launch at scale.

The first economic unlock could be local material use, then cislunar construction feedstock. Lunar escape does not automatically produce a stable useful Earth orbit. Capture, guidance, trajectory losses, return hardware and fleet operations determine delivered cost. The earlier [lunar mass-driver study](../orbits-and-lunar-resources/lunar-mass-driver-research-report.md) analyzes these delivery-chain issues in more detail.

My preferred planning sequence is prospecting and repeatable landings; power and dust-resistant handling; local oxygen/metals and construction; quality-controlled components; a small launcher/capture demonstration; then bulk exports. Cheap Earth launch helps deliver the factory while also strengthening its competition. Lunar production wins only when its full operating cost beats an increasingly capable Earth supply chain.

## Earth-derived boost and propulsion wild cards

There are two different questions: launch from Earth's surface, and departure from Earth orbit. The second admits low-thrust or externally powered technologies that cannot lift off from the first.

For a nonrelativistic ideal jet:

```text
F = mass_flow × exhaust_velocity
P_jet = F × exhaust_velocity / 2
F = 2 × efficiency × input_power / exhaust_velocity
```

At fixed input power, raising exhaust velocity lowers thrust. At fixed thrust, it raises power. A vehicle merely supporting Earth weight needs P_jet / vehicle_mass = g × exhaust_velocity / 2. At 30 km/s this is **147 kW/kg of total vehicle mass**, or 147 GW for 1,000 tonnes. Liftoff needs margin above weight, and real losses increase source power. Compactness alone does not solve this requirement.

An ideal rocket-equation illustration with 9.4 km/s assigned ascent delta-v gives mass ratios **8.08 at 4.5 km/s exhaust**, **2.84 at 9 km/s**, and **1.37 at 30 km/s**. These ratios include dry vehicle and payload together as final mass; they omit explicit staging, engine/tank design and trajectory optimization. Actual gravity losses depend on thrust. The tempting high-exhaust-velocity result must be purchased with an engine capable of the required power and thrust.

| Candidate | Useful possibility | What prevents treating it as a scheduled unlock |
|---|---|---|
| Reusability, operations, depots, cryogenic management | Large gain in Earth-supplied useful mass and cost without radically higher exhaust velocity | Cadence, turnaround, boiloff, transfer and demand must be demonstrated economically. Strongest near-term planning basis. |
| Solid-core nuclear thermal | Approximately 8–10 km/s hydrogen exhaust for high-thrust in-space stages | Engine qualification, tanks, reactor life and mission economics. Orbital use is the much stronger near-term case. |
| Laser/microwave thermal | Keep much of the power-generation mass off the vehicle; potentially improve departure propulsion | Large beam infrastructure, atmosphere, pointing, receiver/chamber survival, and route coverage. |
| Advanced thermal fission, including gas core | Higher gas temperature and exhaust velocity while retaining useful thrust | Fuel containment, thermal radiation, materials and integrated operation. No required chronological position relative to fusion. |
| Fission fragments | Directly use energetic charged fragments; potentially very high exhaust velocity | Fragment escape, criticality, useful thrust, residual heat and complete engine mass. Primarily an in-space concept. |
| FRC, mirror or pulsed direct fusion | Potentially better propulsion geometry than a closed toroidal power station | Net energy, sustained or repeated confinement, nozzle coupling, radiation and specific power. |
| Tethers or electromagnetic launch assistance | Reduce propellant burden by exchanging momentum or supplying launch energy externally | Large structures, rendezvous/traffic, reboost, acceleration and atmospheric passage. Infrastructure substitutes for some rocket performance. |

The [McGill laser-thermal mission study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.00244) models roughly 3,000-s specific impulse and a 45-day Mars trip using a 100-MW-class beam. It starts from Earth orbit and confronts difficult Mars capture. This is not demonstrated surface-to-orbit transport. NASA's [fission-fragment NIAC study](https://www.nasa.gov/directorates/spacetech/niac/2023/Aerogel_Core_Fission_Fragment_Rocket_Engine/) proposes specific impulse above 100,000 s; its status is explicitly a speculative concept study, not a prototype result. Likewise the [wave-rotor NTP/NEP proposal](https://www.nasa.gov/general/new-class-of-bimodal-ntp-nep-with-a-wave-rotor-topping-cycle-enabling-fast-transit-to-mars/) warrants tracking without adopting its mission dates as forecasts.

For actual Earth liftoff, my central expectation remains chemical propulsion with better economics for a long time. For Earth's orbital departure, nuclear thermal and electric stages are more credible near-term improvements; beamed thermal propulsion is a particularly interesting infrastructure-dependent alternative. Raising exhaust velocity is only one route to increasing a Terra Invicta-like Boost capacity.

## What to watch through 2031

| Observable milestone | Why it should change the forecast |
|---|---|
| Space reactor passes integrated endurance testing and reaches flight integration with fuel and launch readiness | Stronger evidence than a design release or target year. |
| Reactor starts and runs in space with useful load and measured degradation | Reduces environmental and integration uncertainties. |
| Fusion plant publishes complete net energy accounting over representative duty | Separates plasma success from useful electrical delivery. |
| D-T program measures breeding, recovery and inventory through an integrated fuel cycle | Tests whether fleet growth can sustain its fuel supply. |
| Irradiated components survive useful service, are replaced remotely, and return to operation | Constrains availability and lifecycle economics. |
| Electric thruster demonstrates required total impulse and lifetime at high power | Separates a brief power test from a transport engine. |
| Lunar extraction runs repeatedly on the Moon with measured energy, uptime and maintenance | Converts simulant chemistry into an operating production process. |
| Mass-driver program publishes payload, acceleration, destination/capture architecture and funded hardware milestones | Converts a strategic aspiration into a testable development plan. |

For technology progression, track **complete-system specific power, exhaust velocity, thrust, duty cycle, lifetime, manufacturing scale and logistics** separately. A fission/fusion label does not establish all seven. By 2100 the consequential achievement may be a maintained, replicable industrial network that supports several propulsion families, rather than a single ultimate reactor type.

## Reproducible calculation notes

All numeric examples above use SI units, g = 9.80665 m/s², Stefan–Boltzmann constant 5.670374419e-8 W m^-2 K^-4, lunar example speed 2,380 m/s, and 86,400 seconds/day.

```text
Mass ratio = exp(9400 / exhaust_velocity)
Earth weight-support jet W/kg = 9.80665 × exhaust_velocity / 2
Radiator emitting area = waste_heat / (0.9 × 5.670374419e-8 × temperature^4)
Launch kWh/kg = (0.5 × 2380^2) / 3.6e6
Track length = 2380^2 / (2 × acceleration)
Illustrative tonnes/day = 100000 × 86400 × 0.5 / (0.5 × 2380^2) / 1000
```

The calculations were evaluated independently in PowerShell. No simulations of a complete reactor, trajectory or industrial facility were performed. Existing gameplay files and other ongoing repository changes were not part of this research.
